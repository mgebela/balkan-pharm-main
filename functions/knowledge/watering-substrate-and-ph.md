# Watering, substrate and pH

More first-time grows are damaged by watering habits than by any pest or deficiency. Roots
need water *and* air; the skill is supplying one without excluding the other.

## How to water correctly

Water by **weight and dryness, not by schedule.** A fixed "every two days" routine ignores
plant size, pot size, temperature and humidity, all of which change constantly.

- Lift the pot. A watered pot is obviously heavy; a dry one is surprisingly light. This is the
  single most reliable indicator and costs nothing.
- Or push a finger 3–4 cm into the medium. Dry at that depth means water; damp means wait.
- Water until **10–20% runs out of the drainage holes.** That runoff flushes accumulated
  salts and confirms the whole root ball was wetted, not just the surface.
- Then let it dry appreciably before the next watering. The wet/dry cycle is what pulls fresh
  air into the root zone and drives root growth.
- Water in the **early morning** outdoors, so foliage dries before night and the plant has
  water available before the heat of the day.

Small plants in big pots are the classic trap: the plant cannot drink the volume, the medium
stays wet for days, and the roots suffocate. Pot up gradually instead.

## Overwatering vs underwatering

They look alike — both droop — but the feel differs:

- **Overwatered**: leaves are heavy, firm, curled down from the stem, dark green. Medium is
  damp. Drooping does not improve after watering; it worsens. Often accompanied by fungus
  gnats and a sour smell.
- **Underwatered**: leaves are thin, papery, limp, hanging from weak stems. Pot is light.
  Recovery after watering is dramatic, often within an hour or two.

Overwatering is far more common and far more dangerous, because sustained saturation causes
root rot, which then looks exactly like a nutrient deficiency and gets "fixed" with more
feed, making it worse. If leaves are drooping and the pot is heavy, do not water.

## pH: the reason nutrients "disappear"

Nutrients are only available to roots inside a pH band. Outside it they are physically
present but chemically locked out, so the plant shows a textbook deficiency while the
substrate is full of the element.

Target ranges:
- **Soil / organic**: 6.0–7.0, ideally around 6.3–6.8.
- **Coco coir**: 5.5–6.5, ideally around 5.8–6.2.
- **Hydroponic**: 5.5–6.2.

Always check pH **before** treating a suspected deficiency. Adding more of a locked-out
nutrient raises salt levels without fixing anything.

Measure the **runoff** as well as the input: input pH tells you what you gave, runoff tells
you what the root zone is actually experiencing. A large gap between them means the substrate
is drifting and needs flushing.

## EC, runoff and salt build-up

EC (electrical conductivity) measures dissolved salts — roughly, feed strength.

- Rising runoff EC over successive waterings means salts are accumulating faster than the
  plant uses them. Reduce feed strength or water with plain pH-adjusted water for a cycle.
- Falling runoff EC means the plant is consuming more than you are supplying and can usually
  take a stronger feed.
- Crusty white deposits on the medium surface or pot rim are visible salt build-up.
- Symptoms of excess salts — burnt leaf tips, dark green clawing leaves — are frequently
  misdiagnosed as a deficiency and treated with more feed.

## Choosing a substrate

- **Quality potting soil with compost**: the most forgiving. Holds a nutrient buffer, so pH
  swings and small feeding mistakes are absorbed. Best choice for a first grow.
- **Coco coir**: fast-draining and high-oxygen, allowing aggressive growth, but it is inert
  and buffers nothing — every watering must be a correctly balanced feed, and it needs
  calcium and magnesium supplementation. Less forgiving of neglect.
- **Amended "living" soil**: a mature soil with worm castings, compost and slow-release
  amendments feeds the plant biologically. Very low maintenance once established, but slow to
  correct if it goes wrong.
- **Perlite** at 20–30% by volume improves aeration in almost any mix and is the cheapest fix
  for a medium that stays wet too long.

## Pot size and root health

- Roots define the canopy. A plant restricted to a small pot stays small no matter how well
  it is fed.
- Rough guide: 5–10 L for a small autoflower, 15–30 L for a mid-size photoperiod, 50 L+ for a
  large outdoor plant. Outdoors, planting directly into amended ground beats any pot.
- **Fabric pots** air-prune roots at the edges, producing a dense fibrous root mass instead of
  a spiralling root-bound one, and they drain and breathe better. They dry out faster, so
  expect to water more often.
- Transplant before the plant becomes root-bound — circling roots at the drainage holes mean
  you are already late. Transplant into moist (not soaked) medium and avoid disturbing the
  root ball.
- Autoflowers dislike transplanting; the check to growth costs them yield they cannot recover
  by vegetating longer. Sow them into their final pot.

## Water quality

- Very hard tap water is already high in calcium and magnesium; adding a Cal/Mag supplement
  on top can push it into excess.
- Very soft or reverse-osmosis water contains nothing, so calcium and magnesium must be
  supplied deliberately — RO users often see Cal/Mag deficiencies.
- Chlorinated tap water is generally fine for plants but suppresses soil biology; standing it
  uncovered for 24 hours lets chlorine off-gas. Chloramine does not off-gas.
- Cold water shocks roots. Aim for room temperature, roughly 18–22 °C.
