# Light, training and canopy management

Yield is mostly a function of how much light lands on flower sites. Training is simply the
work of turning one dominant tip into many well-lit ones.

## Light intensity by stage (indoor)

PPFD is the light actually reaching the canopy, measured in µmol/m²/s. Approximate targets:

- **Seedling**: 200–300. Fresh seedlings bleach easily under full-power lamps.
- **Vegetative**: 400–600.
- **Flowering**: 700–900. Above roughly 1000, extra light stops paying unless CO₂ is
  supplemented, and light-burn risk rises sharply.

Without a meter, use the manufacturer's hanging distance and watch the plant. **Light burn**
looks like bleached, yellow-white upper leaves directly under the lamp while lower leaves stay
healthy — distinguishing it from a nutrient problem, which usually starts lower down.

Distance matters more than wattage. Raising a lamp 10 cm can drop intensity substantially,
which is why the top colas burn while the rest of the plant looks fine.

## Photoperiod: the light schedule

- **Vegetative**: 18/6 is the common standard. 24/0 gives marginally faster growth but no
  dark-period recovery and higher running cost; most growers prefer 18/6.
- **Flowering**: **12/12**, and the dark period must be genuinely dark and uninterrupted.
  Light leaks during the dark period are a leading cause of hermaphroditism and of plants
  reverting to vegetative growth.
- **Autoflowers** ignore this entirely — they flower on age. Many growers run them at 18/6 or
  20/4 for their whole life, since more light hours simply means more photosynthesis.

Switch a photoperiod plant to 12/12 when it has reached roughly **half to two-thirds** of the
final size you want. Most cultivars roughly double in height after the flip — the "stretch" —
over the first 2–3 weeks of flowering.

## Low-stress training (LST)

Bending and tying branches rather than cutting them. The lowest-risk, highest-return technique
available, and it suits almost every situation.

- Bend the main stem sideways and tie it down. The plant responds by pushing the lower side
  branches upward, converting one tall cola into many equal ones.
- Do it gradually, on young flexible growth. Older stems snap — although a partially snapped
  stem taped up will usually heal into a thicker one.
- Aim for a **flat, even canopy**: every growing tip at roughly the same height receives
  roughly the same light.
- LST works on autoflowers, where cutting techniques are risky, because it costs no recovery
  time.

## Topping, FIMing and pruning

- **Topping**: cut the main growing tip above a node. The plant produces two main stems
  instead of one. Repeat for 4, 8 and so on. Do it during vegetative growth only, and allow
  several days of recovery.
- **FIM**: an intentionally incomplete top, often producing 3–4 tips. Less predictable.
- **Lollipopping**: remove the lowest third of growth — the small shaded branches that would
  only ever make wispy flowers. Concentrates energy in the canopy and dramatically improves
  airflow underneath, which matters greatly in humid climates.
- **Defoliation**: selectively removing large fan leaves that shade flower sites. Effective in
  moderation; damaging when overdone. Leaves are the plant's engine, not decoration.

Timing rule: finish heavy training **before flowering** or in the first 2 weeks of it. After
about week 3 of flowering, the plant is committed and cannot recover — cutting then simply
costs yield. Autoflowers should not be topped unless they are visibly vigorous and ahead of
schedule; they have no time to recover.

## SCROG and SOG

- **SCROG (screen of green)**: a net stretched above the canopy; growing tips are woven
  through it and kept level. Produces a very even, very well-lit canopy and excellent yield
  per lamp. Requires a longer vegetative period and consistent weekly attention.
- **SOG (sea of green)**: many small plants flowered early with a single main cola each.
  Fastest cycle time, minimal training, but needs many plants — often the limiting factor
  where plant counts are legally capped.

## Airflow and canopy hygiene

Airflow is a cultivation input, not a comfort feature:

- Moving air strengthens stems, keeps humidity from sitting inside dense flowers, and makes
  it far harder for mould and mites to establish.
- Aim for gentle, constant movement — leaves rustling, not thrashing. Wind burn is real.
- Keep the space under the canopy open. Still, humid air trapped beneath dense lower growth
  is where botrytis and powdery mildew begin.
- Extract air rather than only circulating it. In flowering, replacing the air in the room
  every few minutes controls both humidity and temperature.

## Temperature, humidity and VPD

Rough targets by stage:

- **Seedling**: 22–26 °C, 60–70% RH.
- **Vegetative**: 22–28 °C, 40–60% RH.
- **Flowering**: 20–26 °C, 40–50% RH, dropping toward 40% in late flower to limit rot.

VPD (vapour pressure deficit) combines temperature and humidity into a single figure for how
strongly the air pulls moisture from the leaf. Useful ranges are roughly 0.4–0.8 kPa for
seedlings, 0.8–1.2 in vegetative growth, and 1.2–1.6 in flower. Low VPD stalls transpiration
and nutrient uptake; high VPD closes stomata and stresses the plant.

A **cooler night than day** (about 5–8 °C difference) in late flower encourages colour and
aroma development. Sustained temperatures above roughly 30 °C degrade terpenes and, in dense
flowers, encourage loose airy growth.
