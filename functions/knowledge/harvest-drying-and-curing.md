# Harvest, drying and curing

Everything gained over months of growing can be lost in the two weeks after cutting. Drying
and curing are not an afterthought — they are the last and least forgiving stage.

## Knowing when to harvest

Pistils (the hairs) are a weak signal; **trichomes are the real one.** Use a jeweller's loupe
or a cheap USB microscope and look at the resin glands on the flower itself, not on the sugar
leaves, which ripen earlier and will mislead you.

- **Clear / transparent**: not ready. Harvesting now gives low potency and a thin effect.
- **Cloudy / milky**: peak cannabinoid content. Most growers target largely cloudy.
- **Amber**: THC is degrading to CBN. Effect becomes heavier and more sedative.

Common targets: ~70% cloudy with 10–30% amber for a balanced result; more amber for a heavier
effect; mostly cloudy with little amber for a brighter one.

Practical notes:
- Check several sites on the plant. The top ripens before the lower branches, which is why
  staggered harvesting over several days often beats cutting everything at once.
- Breeder flowering times are estimates from the *start of flowering*, not from seed, and are
  usually optimistic. Trust the trichomes over the label.
- Outdoors, weather can override ripeness. A forecast of warm humid rain in late flower is a
  reason to harvest now — see bud rot guidance for humid autumns.

## Drying: slow, cool and dark

The target is to remove roughly 75% of the water over **10–14 days**. Faster is always worse.

- **Temperature 18–20 °C, humidity 55–62%, complete darkness, gentle indirect airflow.**
- Never point a fan directly at drying flowers. Air should move *through the room*, not
  across the buds.
- Light degrades cannabinoids; heat drives off terpenes. Both losses are permanent.
- Whole-plant or large-branch drying is slower and more forgiving than drying loose buds,
  because the stems and leaves release moisture gradually.

A dry that finishes in 3–4 days has been rushed. The outside dries while the inside stays wet,
the result smells like cut grass (residual chlorophyll), and no cure will fully fix it.

**The stem-snap test**: a small stem should snap cleanly rather than bend. Bending means the
core is still wet. Snapping on the *thickest* stems means you may have over-dried.

## Wet vs dry trimming

- **Wet trim** (immediately after cutting): easier, faster, resin is less likely to be knocked
  off, and it removes leaf that would otherwise hold moisture. Better in humid climates where
  rot risk during the dry is real.
- **Dry trim** (after drying): the surrounding leaf slows the dry and protects the flower,
  usually giving a better final aroma. Better in dry climates, where an unprotected bud would
  dry too fast.

Choose by your ambient humidity: humid room, trim wet; dry room, trim dry.

## Curing

Curing continues after the dry and is where aroma actually develops. Chlorophyll and residual
sugars break down, harshness fades, and terpene character emerges.

- Place dried flower in **airtight glass jars, filled about 70–75%** — enough air exchange,
  not enough to dry out.
- Target **58–62% relative humidity** inside the jar. A small hygrometer in one jar is the
  cheapest quality-control tool available.
- **Burp** the jars: open for 10–15 minutes once or twice daily for the first week, then once
  every few days in week two, then weekly.
- Store cool and dark. Heat and light undo the cure.

Most improvement occurs in the **first 2–4 weeks**; many cultivars keep improving for 2–3
months. Curing cannot rescue a bad dry, but a good cure clearly separates good flower from
merely dried flower.

## Problems during dry and cure

- **Ammonia smell in the jar**: anaerobic bacteria — the flower went in too wet. Remove
  immediately, dry further in open air for several hours, then re-jar. Do not ignore it.
- **Visible mould or a musty smell**: discard the affected material. Mouldy flower is a
  genuine health hazard and cannot be salvaged by drying.
- **Over-dried and brittle**: add a humidity-control pack, or a single fresh flower in the jar
  for a few hours, then remove it. Never use fruit peel — it introduces mould.
- **Hay or fresh-cut-grass smell**: dried too fast and too warm; chlorophyll never broke down.
  An extended cure helps but rarely fully corrects it.
- **Harsh smoke despite a good cure**: often excess nitrogen carried into late flower rather
  than a curing fault.

## Storage

- Airtight glass, cool, dark, 58–62% RH. Avoid plastic bags — they build static, strip
  trichomes and breathe unpredictably.
- Cannabinoids degrade with time, heat, light and oxygen. THC slowly converts to CBN, giving
  a more sedative, less bright effect.
- Well-cured flower held properly keeps good quality for 6–12 months, and remains usable well
  beyond that with gradual loss of aroma.
- Freezing whole dried flower makes trichomes brittle and they break off on handling; freeze
  only if the material is destined for extraction.
