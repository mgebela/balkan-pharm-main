# CBD, THC, chemotypes and legal thresholds

What a plant *can* produce is set by genetics; what it *does* produce is set by how well you
grow it and when you harvest. Understanding the difference prevents both disappointment and
compliance failures.

## Chemotypes: what decides CBD vs THC

Both THCA and CBDA are made from the same precursor, CBGA. Which enzyme the plant carries
decides where that precursor goes, and that is fixed at the seed:

- **Type I — THC-dominant.** High THC, minimal CBD. Standard drug-type cannabis.
- **Type II — balanced.** Roughly 1:1 CBD:THC. Common in medical cultivars.
- **Type III — CBD-dominant.** High CBD with THC typically under 0.3%. This is what
  "industrial hemp" and most CBD flower is.
- **Type IV — CBG-dominant.** The precursor is largely left unconverted.
- **Type V** — essentially cannabinoid-free, grown for fibre and seed.

The key consequence: **you cannot grow a Type I plant into a CBD plant, or a Type III plant
into a high-THC plant.** Feeding, light, and stress change *total* cannabinoid content, not
the ratio. If you want CBD, you must start from CBD-dominant certified seed.

Note that seed sold as "CBD" from an unstable or poorly bred line can segregate — some plants
come out Type II rather than Type III, which is a common cause of unexpectedly high THC.

## Total THC, decarboxylation, and why lab numbers differ

Fresh plant material contains mostly **THCA**, the acidic form, which is not intoxicating.
Heat converts it to Δ9-THC. Because THCA is a heavier molecule, converting it loses mass:

    Total THC ≈ (THCA × 0.877) + Δ9-THC

Many jurisdictions regulate **total THC** by this formula, not the Δ9-THC a raw sample shows.
A crop that looks compliant on a raw Δ9 reading can fail a total-THC test. Always check which
figure your local rules use before assuming a result is safe.

## Harvest timing drives compliance, not just potency

Cannabinoid concentration climbs through flowering and plateaus near ripeness. The CBD:THC
*ratio* stays roughly constant, but the **absolute** THC number rises with it.

That means a Type III hemp crop testing at 0.2% total THC in week 6 can exceed 0.3% by week
9 — the plant did nothing wrong, it just kept ripening. Crops that cross the limit are
commonly called "hot" and may have to be destroyed.

Practical approach:
- Sample and test **before** you intend to harvest, not after, leaving time to act.
- If the number is climbing towards the limit, harvest earlier. Losing some ripeness is
  cheaper than losing the crop.
- Test a representative sample — upper flowers from several plants — since the top of the
  plant is usually the most concentrated.
- Keep the lab report with the batch. Traceability is usually part of the legal requirement.

## Legal thresholds vary — verify locally

Thresholds and rules differ by country and change over time. As general orientation only:

- The **EU** applies a hemp THC limit of **0.3%** (raised from 0.2% under the current CAP
  regulation), with cultivation restricted to certified varieties from the EU common
  catalogue and usually requiring registration of the parcel with the national authority.
- The **US** federal definition of hemp is **0.3% Δ9-THC on a dry-weight basis**, with state
  programmes adding their own testing and licensing requirements.
- Many countries permit hemp for fibre/seed but not flower production, or license CBD
  extraction separately from cultivation.

This is orientation, not legal advice. Rules on licensing, permitted varieties, THC limits,
testing windows and flower sales change frequently — confirm the current requirements with
your national authority before planting, and keep certified-seed documentation.

## Growing for CBD quality

CBD-dominant crops are grown much like any other cannabis, with a few emphases:

- **Certified seed of a listed variety** is usually both a legal requirement and the only
  reliable way to hit a predictable ratio.
- **Avoid pollination.** Seeded flower loses cannabinoid and terpene content as the plant
  redirects energy into seed. Remove males early unless you are breeding.
- **Do not over-feed nitrogen in late flower.** It delays ripening and leaves a harsh product.
- **Protect the resin.** Trichomes are fragile — handle plants gently in late flower, avoid
  overhead watering onto buds, and do not rub or squeeze flowers when inspecting.
- **Dry slowly and cool.** Terpenes are volatile; a fast hot dry strips the aroma that
  distinguishes good CBD flower from biomass.

## Terpenes and quality beyond cannabinoid percentage

Cannabinoid percentage is a poor proxy for quality. Terpenes drive aroma, flavour, and much
of the perceived character, and they are far easier to destroy than to create:

- Most terpenes are volatile below 25 °C. Drying above roughly 20–21 °C measurably reduces
  them.
- Excessive heat and intense direct light late in flower degrade terpenes in the living plant
  too — a common reason greenhouse flower can out-aroma a hot indoor room.
- Cool nights in late flower (a 5–8 °C day/night difference) tend to improve colour and aroma
  expression, and are one thing an outdoor autumn does naturally well.
- Cure slowly in glass at 58–62% RH. Most aroma development happens in the first 2–4 weeks of
  cure, not during the dry.
