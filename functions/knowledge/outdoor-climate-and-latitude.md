# Outdoor growing by climate and latitude

Outdoors, two things you cannot change decide most of the outcome: your **latitude**, which
sets when photoperiod plants flower, and your **autumn weather**, which decides whether
those flowers finish clean or rot. Pick genetics to fit the season you actually have rather
than fighting the calendar.

## Latitude and the flowering trigger

Photoperiod cannabis starts flowering as nights lengthen — roughly when daylight drops
under about 14 hours. That date depends on latitude, not on temperature:

- **44–47°N** (Croatia, N Italy, S France, Romania): daylight falls under ~14 h in the first
  half of August. Flowering runs August–October; most cultivars finish late September to
  mid-October.
- **50–54°N** (Netherlands, Poland, S UK): trigger lands late July/early August, but autumn
  closes in faster. You need cultivars finishing by late September.
- **38–42°N** (C Italy, Spain, Greece, Turkey): trigger is late August/September and autumn
  is long and dry — the widest choice of late-finishing cultivars.

Practical rule: count backwards from your **first expected frost**. A cultivar needs its
full flowering window (typically 8–11 weeks) to finish before that date. If it does not fit,
choose an earlier-finishing cultivar or an autoflower.

## Continental climate (Croatia, Hungary, Serbia, N Balkans)

Köppen Cfb/Cfa — hot dry midsummer, then a humid, foggy, dewy autumn. This combination is
the defining challenge:

- **July–August**: 30–38 °C highs, low rainfall. Heat stress and drought are the limiters.
  Photosynthesis stalls above roughly 30–32 °C; plants transpire hard and wilt at midday.
  Water deeply in the early morning, mulch heavily, and use light-coloured or fabric pots so
  roots do not cook.
- **September–October**: cool nights, heavy morning dew, fog in river valleys, autumn rain.
  This is **botrytis (bud rot) season** and the single biggest cause of lost outdoor crops
  in the region.

Because the wet period arrives exactly when buds are densest, favour cultivars that finish
by late September, or that have open, airy flower structure. A dense indica-leaning bud in a
Slavonian October will very often rot from the inside.

## Beating autumn bud rot in a humid continental autumn

Botrytis starts inside the bud where you cannot see it, and spreads fastest in still, humid
air at 15–22 °C — exactly a continental September night.

- **Airflow first.** Space plants generously, keep the lower third of the plant stripped of
  leaf and small growth, and never let plants touch each other or a wall.
- **Keep water off flowers.** Rain covers, a hoop tunnel, or a simple lean-to during autumn
  rain pays for itself. Shake heavy water off branches after rain.
- **Inspect every 1–2 days from week 4 of flowering.** Look for a single wilted or bleached
  sugar leaf sticking out of an otherwise healthy bud — that is usually rot underneath.
- **Cut out infected material immediately** with a wide margin, into a bag, away from the
  plant. Do not shake it — spores spread on contact.
- **Harvest early rather than lose it.** A crop taken a week early is worth more than a crop
  lost to rot. If a warm humid spell is forecast in late flower, take the ripest plants.

## Mediterranean and coastal climates

Köppen Csa — hot dry summers, mild dry autumns (Dalmatia, Spain, Greece, S Italy).

- Autumn is far drier, so bud rot pressure is much lower and long-flowering cultivars finish
  well into October or November.
- The limiters shift to **heat, drought, and spider mites**, which thrive in hot dry air.
- Use shade cloth (30–40%) during heat waves, water volume over frequency to drive roots
  deep, and check leaf undersides weekly for mite stippling.
- Coastal salt spray and hard, alkaline irrigation water can push the substrate pH up over a
  season — check runoff pH monthly.

## Short-season and northern climates

Above roughly 52°N, or anywhere with a cold wet autumn and an early frost:

- **Autoflowers are the honest answer.** They flower on age rather than daylight, finishing
  in 10–13 weeks from seed regardless of latitude, so they can be harvested in August before
  the weather turns.
- Two autoflower cycles per season (sow April and again mid-June) often beats one photoperiod
  crop that risks the whole season on October weather.
- Start seedlings indoors and transplant after the last frost; cold soil below ~15 °C stalls
  root growth badly.
- A greenhouse or polytunnel adds 3–5 weeks of usable season at both ends.

## Autoflower vs photoperiod outdoors

- **Autoflower**: ignores daylight, finishes fast, dodges autumn weather, stays small, and
  cannot be topped or vegetated longer to fix a slow start. One shot — a stunted seedling
  stays a small plant. Best for short seasons, rot-prone autumns, and stealth.
- **Photoperiod**: much larger yields, can be trained and vegetated as long as you like, and
  can be re-vegetated or cloned. But it flowers on the calendar's schedule, not yours, so it
  must fit inside your frost window.

Mixed strategy that works well in continental Europe: autoflowers sown in spring for an
August harvest, plus photoperiods for a late-September harvest, spreading weather risk.

## Sun, siting and wind

- Cannabis is a full-sun plant: aim for **6+ hours of direct sun**, ideally south-facing in
  the northern hemisphere.
- Morning sun is worth more than afternoon sun in humid regions — it burns dew off the
  flowers early and cuts rot risk.
- Avoid frost pockets and valley bottoms where cold, damp air settles overnight.
- Shelter from prevailing wind, but not so much that airflow stops. Wind also thickens stems;
  plants grown in dead-still air often need staking.
- Stake or cage before flowering. Wet flowers are extremely heavy and branches snap in the
  first autumn downpour.
